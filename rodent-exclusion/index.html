Where rodents get in.

A house has dozens of small openings, and most are invisible from the ground. The typical entries cluster around utilities, rooflines and doors. A technician walks the exterior with a flashlight and a probe, looking for daylight, gnaw marks and greasy rub smears.

The right sequence.

Sealing follows a sequence. Trapping or confirmation comes first, so no animal is locked in. Large openings are closed next, then small ones, working from the ground up. Finally the technician looks again for fresh signs near each patch.

  • Confirm the animals are out, either by catches or by a quiet trap line.
  • Close ground-level openings first, since they are the most used.
  • Move up to vents, soffits and roof edges.
  • Seal utility penetrations last, using metal collars.
  • Inspect each patch at the recheck.

Why material matters.

Rodent incisors grow continuously and are kept sharp by gnawing. Soft materials give them something to work on. Metal, masonry and cured mortar do not, so a repair made from those lasts for years where a foam bead lasts a few days.

Mesh sized to a quarter inch blocks mice and rats while allowing airflow. Where a code or a product label restricts how much ventilation a vent must keep, the technician matches the opening to the original.

Easy exterior chores.

Trim limbs that reach the roof, since a branch is a bridge. Rake mulch back from the foundation so the sill is visible. Check that the garage door seal sits flat on the floor, and replace it if daylight shows.

Do not stuff holes with paper or rags. A rodent pulls them out within a night, and you lose the evidence of where the animal was working.

Additions, bump-outs and older frames.

Buildings of mixed ages, from the Schenck-Mann House of about 1700 to postwar ranches and newer infill, tend to have joints where old and new work meet. Those seams settle and open over the years, and mice use them. When you describe your house, mention any addition, converted porch or finished garage, because the technician inspects those junctions closely before closing them.

Your side of the doorway.

  • Shut the door to the loudest room and tell the whole household it is off limits until we come.
  • Dry sweeping is out, because bristles fling grit into the air you breathe.
  • Bait left loose on a shelf can send a dying animal into a wall nobody can open, so leave it in the store.
  • Write the clock time beside every thump overhead.

Cleanup rules from public health agencies.

If waiting for the crew is not an option, go through the items in order and stop whenever something seems unclear.

  • Dry droppings crumble easily, which keeps the broom and shop vacuum in the closet.
  • Airing out a closed room for about thirty minutes is the first CDC move.
  • Put gloves on at the beginning, and close out the job with soap and water.
  • Disinfectant needs time to work, so wait several minutes after spraying before wiping.
  • Use a second bag if the first one is thin or heavy.
  • Feeling off afterward? Tell a doctor what the cleanup involved and let the doctor judge.

About closing the gaps.

Why not use expanding foam?

Foam does not last against rodent teeth, and mice sometimes carry it off for nests. It can fill around a metal plug but should never be the barrier.

Is steel wool enough?

It rusts and gets pulled out, so it works only as a filler behind a lasting barrier.

When is sealing done in the job?

Once trapping has shown which entries are active, or once the animals are confirmed gone. Sealing too early can trap them inside.

How large a hole matters?

Mice can use a gap near a pencil's width, rats about a thumb's width. Anything bigger than that deserves attention.

Will sealing change the look of my house?

Usually not. Mesh and flashing sit behind trim, in vents and in corners, and are painted or matched when needed.
