Trees, hedges and the roofline.

Trash cans with loose lids teach rodents to stop by every week. A latch or bungee cord costs next to nothing and breaks the habit.

Rodents often reach the shed, the detached garage or the play structure before they get to the house. Inspect those first.

Why small places still need an address.

A small hamlet next to larger ones can share mail routes and phone prefixes with its neighbors. That means a caller may think they live in Carle Place when the mailing line says another town. Check the ZIP code on your mail against 11514.

Signals that justify dialing.

Calls usually begin with a single odd detail and a nagging worry. The details below turn up again and again.

  • A stain on the ceiling that no leak explains.
  • Chewed wood along a deck rail or shed door.
  • Pellets resting on top of a parked engine.
  • A cabinet stuffed with shredded paper and cloth.

While you wait for the crew.

  • Keep chewed cords, torn bedding and droppings away from crawling babies and curious terriers.
  • Prop a long-closed shed or garage open and let it air out.
  • A trap you set earlier may remain in place until we inspect it.
  • Mention tree work, roof work or storm cleanup from the past few weeks.

A protective checklist for droppings.

Following this routine keeps airborne dust low during the wait.

  • Even a soft dust mop flings old pellets upward, so no dry tool starts the job.
  • Open every opening you can, then spend thirty minutes somewhere else.
  • Put gloves on at the beginning, and close out the job with soap and water.
  • On a shelf dotted with pellets, work from the outer edges inward so none skitter off.
  • Use a second bag if the first one is thin or heavy.
  • Keep an eye on your health for several weeks, and if flu-like symptoms or breathlessness appear, see a doctor.
