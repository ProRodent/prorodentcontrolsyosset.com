What rodents typically damage.

Rodent teeth never stop growing, so gnawing is a lifelong habit. Insulation gets shredded for nests, plastic duct is chewed for passage and wood is gnawed to enlarge a gap. Wire jackets, though less frequent, are the most dangerous target.

Attic insulation.Crushed, soiled or tunneled through.Pull out and replace the affected sections.

The order of repair.

Repairs wait until trapping is done and the entry points are sealed. Otherwise new animals return to the same nest site and undo the work. The technician walks the house after the last recheck, lists what remains damaged and prices each item.

  • Confirm that no animal remains and each opening is closed.
  • Photograph and measure what was damaged.
  • Carry out contaminated material in sealed bags.
  • Put in new insulation, duct or drywall.
  • Close the repaired area with material rodents resist.

Where damage hides.

Most damage sits out of sight: behind a cabinet, above a ceiling tile, inside a wall. A pantry with a hole in the baseboard may lead to a stud bay full of shredded paper. An attic that looks tidy from the hatch may have a tunnel in the insulation near the eaves.

This is why inspection comes first. The inspection page shows how the technician finds the evidence before estimating repairs.

Safety around chewed wiring.

If you see copper through a torn jacket, do not touch the wire and consider switching off the breaker for that circuit. A licensed electrician should handle repairs. We note the location in the report so the electrician knows where to look.

Smell of burning plastic, flickering lights or a tripping breaker are signs to call an electrician right away.

Matching repairs to the age of the house.

A house built around 1700, like the Schenck-Mann House, or a house from the postwar decades or from the last ten years will each use different materials and framing. That is a general point about age, not a claim about your property. When you call, mention the approximate age of the house and any renovations, because old and new materials meet at seams where rodents like to work and where repairs need the most care.

Your side of the doorway.

  • Choose a single room to keep shut overnight.
  • Test whether the dryer vent flap swings closed and the garage door sits flush.
  • Let the dispatcher know if anyone at home has asthma or breathing trouble.
  • Keep your phone audible, since the technician may call before arriving.

CDC method: wet it down first.

The federal pages are summarized loosely here, and a doctor, not a pest company, should answer any health question.

  • Dry droppings crumble easily, which keeps the broom and shop vacuum in the closet.
  • Open every opening you can, then spend thirty minutes somewhere else.
  • Thin disposable gloves suit small messes, while heavier rubber pairs suit big ones and can be rinsed.
  • Soak the spot, leave it alone for a few minutes, then collect it with paper towels bound for the trash.
  • Put soiled towels, any dead animal, and the gloves or tongs you used into one plastic bag, knot it and set it in the household trash.
  • If you fall ill after contact with droppings, that is a medical question, and the clinician needs to know what you handled.

Questions about repairs.

Do you fix electrical wiring?

No. If you see chewed wire with exposed copper, stay clear and call a licensed electrician. We flag the damage and the cause.

When should repairs happen?

After trapping and sealing. Repairing before the entry is closed risks a repeat.

Can chewed insulation stay?

Only if the damage is minor. Thin or compressed insulation loses its value and holds odors.

Will a patch match the wall?

The repair is made flush and primed. Painting is up to you or a painter.

Will my homeowner policy pay for rodent damage?

Many do not. Read the exclusions or ask your carrier before you plan around a payout.

What a repair visit covers.

Take pictures of chewed insulation, flexible duct and cable jackets, and stay clear of any wire where bare copper shows.
